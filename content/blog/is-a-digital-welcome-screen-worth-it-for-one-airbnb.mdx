Search "digital signage for Airbnb" and most of what comes back assumes a building full of units — a shared lobby, dozens of guests cycling through per week, a property manager fielding the same questions on repeat. That math doesn't apply if you're hosting one unit. A single-door host isn't running a lobby with constant foot traffic; the only people who'll ever see the screen are the current guests in that one listing, for the length of their stay.

So the real question for a solo host isn't "how much ad revenue can this screen generate" — it's "does a TV welcome screen do enough for guest experience to justify the cost and setup time, for one unit." Here's the honest breakdown.

## The options, side by side

| Option | Setup effort | Cost | Updates itself? | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Printed binder / laminated sheet | Low (one-time) | ~$10-20 to print/laminate | No — manual reprint for any change | Hosts who rarely change details and don't mind reprinting |
| Free welcome book (PDF/digital) | Low | Free | Easy to edit, but guests must open it on their phone | Hosts who want something better than paper without new hardware |
| TV welcome screen | Moderate (one afternoon) | $10/screen/month software + a $30-50 streaming stick if needed | Yes — edit from your phone, updates live | Hosts who already have a TV and want a self-updating, always-on display |

If you don't currently own a streaming device plugged into the unit's TV, that's the real cost comparison: a laminated sheet is nearly free, a [free welcome book generator](/airbnb-welcome-book-generator) is free and takes ten minutes, and a TV welcome screen is the only option with a recurring cost — $10 per screen per month, in PiAds' case, on top of whatever stick or player you're running it on.

## What a TV screen actually buys you for one unit

A welcome screen earns its cost in a few specific ways, none of them dramatic on their own:

- **Fewer repetitive messages.** Wifi password, checkout time, door code, parking instructions — if guests can glance at the TV instead of texting you, that's real time saved, especially if you self-manage and get these questions on every single stay.
- **A better first impression.** A screen that greets guests by name when they turn on the TV reads as more polished than a stack of paper on the counter, and first impressions move review language ("thoughtful," "well-organized") more than you'd expect.
- **Fewer "how do I use the TV" questions.** Guests who turn on the TV and immediately see useful information, rather than whatever streaming app was left logged in, have one less point of friction on arrival night.
- **A small chance at ad income.** If your listing allows it, local businesses can book approved ad slots on the screen and you keep 70% of every booking — but see the honest math below before counting on this as a reason to set it up.

None of these are revolutionary. They're the kind of small, cumulative details that nudge a 4-star stay toward a 5-star one, which is exactly the review-margin game most hosts are already playing.

## The honest math on ad revenue for one unit

Here's the part most monetization-angle content skips: **a single-unit screen is not a lobby screen.** The multi-unit version of this pitch works because a shared common area sees dozens of different guests per week, which is a real audience local advertisers pay to reach. A single rental's TV is seen only by whoever is currently staying in that one unit — one party, for the length of one stay.

That means the realistic ad-revenue outcome for a single Airbnb is small — plausibly a few dollars a month, not a meaningful income line. If you're a host with one or two listings, don't set up a welcome screen *for* the ad revenue. Set it up for the guest-experience reasons above, and treat any ad income as a minor bonus that might not even cover the $10/screen software cost on its own. Where the ad marketplace genuinely pays off is at scale — buildings and multi-unit properties with real guest volume are better covered in our [guide for short-term rental properties](/blog/digital-signage-for-short-term-rentals).

## When it's genuinely not worth it

To be fair in the other direction: if your unit turns over infrequently, if your guests are mostly long-term stays who don't need daily orientation, or if you're simply not going to keep the content current, a TV screen is overhead you don't need. A laminated sheet or a [free digital welcome book](/airbnb-welcome-book-generator) covers the same information without a monthly line item or a device to maintain. The TV screen earns its cost specifically when you have enough turnover that repetitive guest questions are a real time cost, and you're willing to spend ten minutes updating it occasionally.

## FAQ

**Is a welcome screen worth it for just one Airbnb listing?**
It depends what you're optimizing for. For guest experience and fewer repetitive questions, yes, especially with frequent turnover. For ad income alone, no — a single unit doesn't have the guest volume to generate meaningful advertiser revenue; that part of the pitch is built for multi-unit properties.

**What's cheaper: a printed sign or a TV screen?**
A printed or laminated sheet is cheaper upfront and has no recurring cost, but it can't update itself — every change means reprinting. A free digital welcome book splits the difference: no recurring cost, easy to edit, but guests view it on their phone rather than the in-room TV.

**Can I use the TV that's already in the unit?**
Usually, yes. If it already has a Fire TV Stick, Android TV, or Google TV built in, you can install signage software directly. If it's a Roku or an older "dumb" TV, you'll need to add a separate streaming stick on another HDMI input.

**Will guests actually pay attention to it?**
Most guests check it once or twice — on arrival, to find the wifi password, and maybe before checkout. It's not meant to hold attention like a lobby screen; it's meant to answer the handful of questions every guest has, the moment they have them.

**Do I need to allow ads to make this worth it?**
No. You can run the screen purely for guest-facing content — wifi, checkout, local picks — and never enable ad slots at all. Ads are optional, and for a single unit, they're a minor bonus rather than the main reason to set one up.
