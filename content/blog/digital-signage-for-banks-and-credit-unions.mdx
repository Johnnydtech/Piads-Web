Walk into most community bank or credit union branches and the lobby TV is either off, stuck on cable news, or looping the same rate sheet from three promotions ago. That's a missed opportunity. Branch lobbies have exactly the audience advertisers want — adults, mid-transaction, with nothing to do but wait for a teller or loan officer — and most of that screen time is wasted.

This guide covers how banks and credit unions are using digital signage well: what to show, where to put screens, and how a branch can turn idle lobby time into a modest, ongoing revenue line.

## Why bank lobbies are a strong fit for signage

**Dwell time is long and predictable.** A teller line or a wait for a loan officer runs anywhere from 3 to 15 minutes — closer to a [medical or dental waiting room](/digital-signage-for/medical-dental-offices) than a retail counter. Members aren't browsing merchandise or distracted by a menu — they're standing or sitting with nothing else to look at.

**The audience skews toward homeowners, savers, and local residents.** Advertisers who want adults with bank accounts — not just foot traffic off the street — pay more attention to this audience than a typical retail crowd.

**Trust transfers.** A credit union's lobby carries an implicit stamp of approval. A local realtor, insurance agent, or home-improvement contractor advertised there benefits from the same trust members already place in their financial institution — which is also why screening advertisers carefully matters more here than almost anywhere else.

**Compliance makes content planning easier, not harder.** Rate and promotional content already goes through a review process at most institutions. Digital signage fits into that existing workflow better than printed signage, which can't be pulled the moment a rate changes.

## What to actually put on the screen

### 1. Rate and product updates
CD rates, mortgage rates, HELOC promotions, auto loan specials. The single biggest advantage over a printed rate board: it updates in minutes, not after a reprint order.

### 2. Financial literacy content
Short, plain-language explainers — what a credit score actually measures, how overdraft protection works, the difference between a HELOC and a home equity loan. This is content members genuinely want and rarely get elsewhere, and it reinforces the institution's role as an educator, not just a lender.

### 3. Branch and digital banking basics
Mobile deposit instructions, ATM locations, hours for the drive-through vs. the lobby, how to schedule an appointment with a loan officer. This reduces the "quick questions" staff field dozens of times a week.

### 4. Community involvement
Credit unions in particular lean on local identity — sponsorships, scholarship recipients, branch volunteer days. This content performs well because it's specific to the community the branch actually serves, not generic brand messaging.

### 5. Local advertiser content
This is the part most branches leave on the table entirely. A member standing in a teller line is a strong prospect for:

- **Local real estate agents** — especially relevant next to a mortgage rate slide
- **Home improvement and contracting businesses** — tied naturally to HELOC and home equity content
- **Insurance agents** — auto, home, and life insurance all pair naturally with a banking audience
- **Financial advisors and CPAs** (where compliance allows) — local, not competing institutions
- **Local retailers and restaurants** — general community advertisers who simply want access to adults with disposable income

Every ad still needs sign-off from the branch before it runs, same as any other venue — which matters more here given how much trust is riding on what appears next to the institution's own rate sheet.

## Screen placement

| Location | Best Content | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Teller line / main lobby | Rates, financial literacy, local ads | Longest, most predictable dwell time |
| Loan officer waiting area | Product deep-dives, advertiser content | Members already in a buying mindset |
| Drive-through monitor | Short rate/promo loops only | Seconds of attention, not minutes |
| Entrance/vestibule | Hours, ATM availability, branch news | Quick glance, not a sit-and-watch screen |

One screen in the main lobby covers most branches. Larger branches with separate loan departments benefit from a second screen there, playing a different, more product-focused rotation.

## Setup

Branch IT departments are often cautious about new hardware on the network, which makes the hardware choice matter more here than at a typical small business. A [Fire TV Stick](/players/fire-tv) or a browser-based web player running on an existing lobby PC both avoid adding unmanaged devices to a sensitive network — see the full [device lineup](/devices) and ask IT before mounting anything new. From there, setup follows the same [guided flow](/blog/fire-tv-stick-digital-signage-setup-guide) as any venue: pair the screen with a short code, build a playlist, and publish.

## Honest trade-offs

If a branch only wants to display rates and hours, a printed rate card and a static display will do that job for less money and zero ongoing management. Digital signage earns its cost when a branch wants to rotate content faster than print allows, run financial literacy segments, or — the part that actually pays for itself — sell ad space to local businesses that want a trusted, captive audience. A single-screen branch with light foot traffic may not generate enough advertiser interest to make that third use case worthwhile; a busy branch with a steady lobby line almost always will.

## FAQ

**Will regulators have a problem with ads running in a bank lobby?**
Advertising space itself isn't typically the issue — financial promotions and disclosures are what's regulated. Keep the institution's own rate and product content compliant as usual, and keep third-party local ads clearly separate and unrelated to financial products the branch itself offers, unless compliance has signed off on a specific arrangement.

**Do members actually pay attention to lobby screens, or do they just stare at their phones?**
More than you'd expect — teller lines move in short bursts, which interrupts phone use more than a longer wait would. Content that's short, visual, and updates frequently holds attention better than a wall of text.

**How much can a single branch realistically earn from local ads?**
It depends heavily on branch traffic and the local advertiser market, but it's a modest, steady supplement, not a primary revenue line — think of it the way a branch would think of ATM surcharge income: real, but not something to build a budget around.
