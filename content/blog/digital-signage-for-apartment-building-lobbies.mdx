Walk through the lobby of most apartment buildings and you'll find one of two things on the TV screen: a muted cable news channel nobody asked for, or nothing at all.

That screen is real estate. And in a building with 100, 200, or 400 units, it's some of the most valuable screen real estate in the city — because every single resident walks past it at least once a day.

Property managers and building owners are starting to realize what retailers, gyms, and restaurants figured out years ago: a screen in front of a captive audience is a business asset. Here's how to put it to work.

## Why Apartment Lobbies Are Exceptional Signage Venues

Most digital signage venues struggle to build audience consistency. A coffee shop sees the same faces a few times a week. A gym gets regulars 3-5 times a week on average. A restaurant might see the same diner once a month.

Your lobby? Every resident passes through it every single day. Often twice.

That level of frequency is unusual in any advertising medium. And it comes with some other characteristics that make apartment lobbies particularly valuable:

**Predictable demographics.** You know exactly who lives in your building. In Arlington, that typically means young professionals aged 25-40, dual-income households, above-average income, and high discretionary spending. That's a demographic local businesses will pay to reach consistently.

**Long engagement windows.** People don't race through lobbies. They wait for elevators, check their mailboxes, hold doors open for neighbors, sign for packages. Average lobby dwell time runs 30-90 seconds per visit — long enough to register a message, short enough to stay sharp.

**High frequency without fatigue.** Residents see your screen so often that it becomes part of their mental wallpaper. That's actually an advertiser's dream: frequency builds familiarity, and familiarity drives action. A local pizza place that shows up on your lobby screen every day for a month becomes the place a resident thinks of when they want takeout on Friday.

**A captive and trusting audience.** Residents have a relationship with their building. They trust the information they see in the lobby. A community announcement on the lobby screen gets read. A local advertiser featured in that same screen gets some of that trust by association.

## 6 Things to Put on Your Lobby Screens

### 1. Building Announcements and Maintenance Notices

Right now, how do you tell residents about a water shutoff scheduled for Tuesday morning? Email? Paper flyers taped to the elevator? A lobby screen changes this completely.

Display upcoming maintenance windows, elevator outages, package room hours, pool closure dates, and inspection notices in a rotating slide. Residents who missed the email see it on their way out. Complaints drop because people feel informed rather than blindsided.

### 2. Community Events and Amenity Reminders

Your rooftop terrace, community room, and fitness center are selling points that justify higher rents — but only if residents actually use them. Most don't, because they forget the amenities exist.

A lobby screen running a weekly "What's Happening" slide changes this. Trivia night in the lounge on Thursday. Yoga on the rooftop Saturday morning. The community room is available to reserve for private events. Residents who know about these things use them, and residents who use them stay.

Retention is worth real money. If a lobby screen helps keep even one resident from leaving — at an average turnover cost of $1,000-3,000 in lost rent and prep — it's already paid for itself multiple times over.

### 3. Local Area Guides

New residents are still learning the neighborhood. Even long-term residents miss things. A rotating "Neighborhood Spotlight" slide is genuinely useful: the best coffee shops nearby, farmers market schedule, upcoming local events, transit tips.

This kind of content requires almost no effort to produce and creates a lot of goodwill. Your building becomes the building that *helps residents feel at home* rather than just the building where they sleep.

### 4. Emergency and Weather Alerts

In the DC metro area, this is more valuable than it sounds. Severe weather, Metro service disruptions, road closures during major events — this is real information that residents actually need on a Tuesday morning rush.

A lobby screen with updated weather and transit information earns daily attention. People glance at it deliberately, not just passively. That makes the non-emergency content (and the advertiser content) more likely to land, too.

### 5. Building Highlights and Resident Features

Some buildings use their lobby screens to spotlight long-term residents, share community photos from building events, or promote resident-run businesses. This content takes a bit more curation but pays off in community feeling — something that matters a lot for lease renewals.

This is where the lobby screen stops being a cost center and starts generating revenue.

Your residents — young, employed, spending money in the neighborhood — are exactly who local businesses want to reach. The restaurant two blocks away. The boutique fitness studio around the corner. ## What Makes a Good Lobby Content Loop

For a standard 60-90 second loop, something like this works well:

1. **Current weather and day/date** (10 seconds) — Useful, glanceable, builds the habit of checking the screen
2. **Building announcement or amenity callout** (10-15 seconds) — Whatever's most timely
3. **Neighborhood spotlight or local events** (10 seconds) — The coffee shop with the new seasonal menu, the farmers market dates
4. **Local advertiser ad** (10 seconds) — Revenue-generating and genuinely local
5. **Community content or upcoming building event** (10 seconds) — The thing worth sticking around for

Keep the visual style clean and readable at a glance. Lobby viewers aren't stopping to read — they're absorbing in passing. Large text, simple layouts, and one key message per slide outperform busy, information-dense designs every time.

## A Different Way to Think About Your Lobby

Most property managers think about their lobby purely as a cost: the TV is a utility, content management is a chore, and the whole thing is just another line item.

The buildings getting ahead of this are thinking differently. Your lobby is where you communicate your brand as a building — what kind of community this is, how much you care about the resident experience, what makes living here worth the rent. A well-run lobby screen is one of the cheapest, most visible ways to improve that experience.

And when it's generating $300-700/month in ad revenue on the side, it's not a cost center anymore.
