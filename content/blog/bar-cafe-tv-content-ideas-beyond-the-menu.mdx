Most bars and cafes that install a screen start and stop at the menu. That's a fine reason to buy one — but a menu board that never changes becomes wallpaper within a week. Regulars already know what you serve; the screen's real job is to give them a reason to glance up anyway.

This is a working list of content that earns attention in a bar or cafe specifically, not a generic signage checklist. Some of it doubles as advertising real estate too, which is where the screen starts paying for itself instead of just costing money.

## What actually holds attention in a bar or cafe

| Content type | Best for | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Happy hour countdown | Bars | Urgency drives orders before the clock hits zero |
| Live trivia / bar games | Bars, taprooms | Gives people a reason to look up between rounds |
| Game day scores & schedule | Sports bars | The screen becomes the reason to stay for one more |
| Dayparted drink menu | Cafes, bars | Right offer at the right hour beats a static list |
| Local weather + suggestion | Cafes | "Cold out — try our hot toddy" feels timely, not automated |
| Staff picks / barista specials | Cafes | Personal recommendation outperforms generic promos |
| Customer photos / social feed | Both | Social proof, and people love seeing themselves on screen |
| Local business ads | Both | Revenue share — this is the one that pays you back |

## 1. Happy hour and last-call countdowns

A literal ticking clock — "Happy hour ends in 0:42:15" — creates urgency a printed sign never can. Run it 45 minutes before happy hour ends and watch order pace pick up. The same trick works for last call, trivia night start time, or a flash discount on a slow Tuesday.

## 2. Trivia, bar games, and live leaderboards

If you run a trivia night, put the current standings on screen between questions. If you don't, a rotating "guess the answer" slide or a simple bar-game leaderboard (cornhole, darts, pool) gives regulars something to argue about — and something to check the screen for.

## 3. Game day: scores, standings, and what's on next

Sports bars live and die by this one. A screen showing today's schedule, the current score of the game playing on the other TVs, or standings for a local fantasy league keeps people in their seats between plays. It doesn't replace the game broadcast — it's the second screen that fills the gaps.

## 4. Dayparted drink and food specials

A cafe's 7am screen and its 4pm screen shouldn't look the same. Morning is coffee, pastries, and the breakfast combo; afternoon is cold brew, snacks, and the after-school rush; evening (if you serve it) shifts to wine or a nightcap menu. ## 5. Weather-triggered suggestions

"It's 38°F outside — try our hot toddy" or "89° and climbing — cold brew's on special" reads as attentive, not automated, and it's a five-minute setup. It also does something subtle: seeing accurate, current weather on screen signals to customers that everything else on the loop is current too.

## 6. Staff picks and barista/bartender specials

A slide with a photo, a name, and a genuine recommendation ("Maria's pick: the espresso tonic — it's not on the printed menu") outperforms a generic promo because it reads as a real person's opinion, not an ad. It's also free content — a phone photo and thirty seconds of writing.

## 7. Customer photos and social proof

Pull in tagged Instagram photos or a curated feed of reviews. People enjoy seeing themselves (or their drink) on the big screen, and it nudges other customers to post too. Curate before it goes live — you want the good photos, not every tagged post.

## 8. Local event and community listings

A bar or cafe screen is one of the most-seen bulletin boards in the neighborhood. Trivia night, live music, a local 5K, a farmers market two blocks over — posting community events (not just your own) makes the venue feel like a hub, and it's content you don't have to create from scratch.

## 9. New menu items and limited runs

A seasonal latte, a guest tap, a one-night cocktail special — anything limited-time deserves screen space specifically because it's temporary. "Back next fall" creates more urgency than a menu item that's always been there.

## 10. QR codes with an actual reason to scan

A QR code with no context gets ignored. "Scan for 10% off your next visit" or "Scan to join trivia night text alerts" gives people a reason. Keep the code large and test it on your own phone before it goes live — a code too small to scan from a barstool is worse than no code at all.

## 11. A quiet rotation of local business ads

This is the one most bars and cafes leave on the table. Once a loop is built, unused slide time can carry ads from other local businesses — a nearby gym, salon, or shop — and every one of them requires your approval before it airs. ## 12. The occasional dead-simple slide

Not everything needs to be clever. A clean photo of the signature cocktail, or the espresso machine mid-pour, with no text at all, is a fine slide to break up a loop that's otherwise all text and countdowns. Give the eye somewhere to rest.

## Building the loop

A 2–3 minute rotation covering 8–10 slides is enough for most single-screen bars and cafes — long enough to feel varied, short enough that a slide reappears within a few minutes for anyone still seated. Mix it roughly: menu/specials (30%), social proof and community content (30%), urgency content like countdowns or limited runs (20%), and local ads (20%). If the loop is only ever promotional, people stop watching it — the non-promotional slides are what earn attention for the ones that sell something.

## FAQ

**Do I still need a separate menu board if I'm doing all this?**
Most bars and cafes run one screen as the primary menu and, if they have a second screen, use it for the rotation above. On a single screen, the menu can be one slide in the loop rather than the only thing on it — dayparted, with specials worked in.

**How often should content actually change?**
Countdowns and game scores update themselves. Everything else — staff picks, event listings, social proof — should get a fresh pass weekly at minimum. Stale content (a "today's special" from three weeks ago) reads worse than no signage at all.

**Will running local ads make my venue feel less personal?**
Not if the venue approves what airs. Every ad on a PiAds screen goes through venue approval first, and ads only fill the slots a venue allows — a couple of slides in an otherwise venue-content loop, not a takeover.

**What if I only have one screen and limited content to fill it?**
Start with three things: the dayparted menu, one piece of social proof, and one urgency slide (happy hour or a limited item). Add trivia, event listings, or ads as the loop grows — a screen doesn't need ten content types on day one.

**Does this work on the TV I already have behind the bar?**
Usually, yes — PiAds runs on Fire TV, Android, Raspberry Pi, or straight in a browser with no extra hardware, so an existing bar TV or a spare tablet can usually be pressed into service without buying anything new.
