Most Airbnb welcome screens stop at two slides: the wifi password and the checkout time. That covers the two questions every host gets asked, but it leaves a TV sitting in front of guests for their entire stay showing almost nothing. A welcome screen that actually earns its spot does more — it answers the questions guests haven't asked yet, makes the property feel more thought-through, and in some cases quietly pays for itself.

This is a working list, organized by when in the stay each type of content matters most.

## Content ideas by stage of stay

| Stage | Content | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival | Wifi, door code, parking | The two or three things guests check within the first five minutes |
| Arrival | Welcome message with guest's name | Small personal touch that costs nothing to set up |
| During stay | Local recommendations | Guests are actively deciding where to eat, what to do |
| During stay | House rules and quiet hours | Easier to absorb from a screen than a printed packet nobody reads |
| During stay | Local event listings | Turns the property into more than a place to sleep |
| During stay | Local business ads (if enabled) | Only applies if you've opted into the ad marketplace |
| Checkout | Checkout time and instructions | The second most-asked question after wifi |
| Checkout | Review request reminder | Timed right, this is the highest-leverage slide in the loop |

## 1. Wifi network and password

The single most-requested piece of information. Use large, legible text — guests are often typing this in from across the room — and keep it on its own slide rather than crammed next to other details.

## 2. A welcome message with the guest's name

If your booking system or PiAds integration can pull the guest's name, a simple "Welcome, Sarah!" on the first slide they see does more for first impressions than almost anything else on this list, for effectively zero extra work once it's set up.

## 3. Door codes, parking, and arrival logistics

Anything a guest needs within their first ten minutes — gate codes, which parking spot is theirs, trash can location, how to work a tricky lock — belongs here. This is the content most likely to turn into a 11pm text message if it's missing.

## 4. House rules and quiet hours

Printed house-rule packets rarely get read cover to cover. A screen that cycles through the two or three rules that actually matter — quiet hours, no-smoking policy, pet policy — gets seen simply because the TV is already on.

## 5. Checkout time and instructions

Checkout time, plus anything guests need to do before leaving (take out trash, lock up, where to leave keys). Put this on the loop throughout the stay, not just the final morning — guests forget checkout time more often than hosts expect.

## 6. Curated local restaurant picks

Not a generic "top 10 restaurants near me" list — your own picks, with a one-line reason each ("best breakfast within walking distance," "go here if it's date night"). Personal curation reads as more trustworthy than algorithmic recommendations, and it's content guests genuinely use.

## 7. Coffee shops and quick-bite options

A separate, smaller category from full restaurant picks — guests often want this answered fast, especially on morning one before they've found their bearings.

## 8. Grocery, pharmacy, and convenience basics

Boring but valuable: nearest grocery store, pharmacy, and a 24-hour option if one exists nearby. Guests who forgot a toothbrush or need allergy medicine at 10pm will remember a host who made this easy to find.

## 9. Transit, parking, and getting around

Nearest transit stop, rideshare pickup tips specific to your building, bike share locations, or parking rules if street parking is involved. This is especially valuable for guests arriving from the airport who haven't rented a car.

## 10. Things to do nearby

Parks, trails, museums, viewpoints — whatever your neighborhood actually offers. This is where a welcome screen starts doing double duty as a mini concierge rather than just an information board.

## 11. Local events happening during their stay

This is the slide that separates a good welcome screen from a great one. A farmers market, a live music night, a seasonal festival — "while you're here" content makes the property feel like it's actively looking out for the guest, not just renting them a room.

## 12. Seasonal or weather-aware tips

"Pool closes for the season November 1st" or "bring a jacket — it cools off fast after sunset here" reads as attentive rather than automated, and it's easy to update a few times a year as seasons change.

## 13. Upsells and partner offers

Early check-in, late checkout, a local bike rental partnership, an airport shuttle — most guests don't ask about add-ons because they don't know they exist. One slide putting the option in front of them, at the moment they're deciding how to spend their stay, captures revenue that a static listing page never surfaces.

## 14. A review request, timed for checkout morning

The highest-leverage slide on this entire list. A short, friendly reminder — "Enjoyed your stay? A review helps us a lot" — showing on checkout morning catches guests while the stay is still fresh, rather than relying on Airbnb's own post-stay email days later.

## Building the rotation

You don't need all fifteen running at once, and a loop that's too long means guests stop watching before it repeats. A reasonable starting rotation is 6-8 slides on a 60-90 second loop: wifi and arrival logistics up front, local recommendations and one "things to do" slide in the middle, house rules and checkout info mixed in, and the review-request slide weighted to show more often as checkout approaches. ## FAQ

**How many slides should an Airbnb welcome screen have?**
6-8 is a good starting point for most single units — enough to feel complete without the loop running so long that guests stop watching before it repeats.

**What's the single most important slide?**
Wifi and door code/parking information for arrival, and the review request for checkout. Those two bookend the stay and get the most actual use.

**Should I put local ads on my welcome screen?**
Only if you want to — it's entirely optional. For a single unit, expect any ad income to be a small bonus rather than meaningful revenue; the content and guest-experience value is the main reason to run a screen at all.

**How often should I update the content?**
Wifi, checkout, and house rules rarely change. Local recommendations and seasonal tips are worth a refresh every few months, and event listings ideally update as often as there's something new happening nearby.

**Do I need a TV for any of this, or can I use a printed guide instead?**
A screen isn't required — a printed guide or our [free digital welcome book](/airbnb-welcome-book-generator) covers the same information. A TV screen's advantage is that it updates itself and is already on when guests walk in, which a printed sheet can't do.
