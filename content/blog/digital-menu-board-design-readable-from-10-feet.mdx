A digital menu board that nobody can read from the ordering line isn't an upgrade — it's a prettier version of the same problem. Swapping a printed board for a screen fixes update speed, not legibility. Legibility is a design job, and most menu boards fail it in the same handful of ways: text too small, contrast too low, too many items crammed onto one panel.

This is a practical design guide, not a software guide. Whatever you use to run your screen — a Fire TV Stick, an Android box, a browser — these rules apply the same way. The goal is simple: a customer standing at the register, in line, or across the room should be able to read your board without squinting.

## How big does the text actually need to be?

There's a widely used rule of thumb in signage design: for every 10 feet of viewing distance, letters need roughly 1 inch of height to stay comfortably legible. It's not a law of physics, but it's close enough to plan around.

| Viewing distance | Minimum letter height | Rough font size (1920×1080 canvas, ~50" screen) |
|---|---|---|
| 3–5 ft (at the counter) | 0.5–1" | 40–60px |
| 8–10 ft (ordering line) | 1–1.5" | 70–100px |
| 15–20 ft (across a dining room) | 2–3" | 120–160px |
| 25+ ft (drive-thru, parking lot) | 3–4"+ | 180px+ |

The practical takeaway: design for your farthest realistic customer, not the one already standing at the register. If your line forms 10 feet back, test your prices and item names from 10 feet back — not from your laptop screen a foot from your face.

## Contrast matters more than font choice

A clean font in low contrast is less readable than a mediocre font in high contrast. The failure mode we see most often is medium-gray text on a slightly-lighter-gray background — it looks "designed" on a laptop and disappears under fluorescent lights or afternoon sun through a window.

Stick to combinations that hold up in a real room: white or near-white text on a dark background, or dark text on a solid light background. Avoid text laid directly over a busy food photo without a solid color block or gradient behind it — the words need a plain surface to sit on, even if that surface is only a strip a few inches tall.

## Fewer items, bigger hierarchy

A common instinct is to fit the whole menu on one screen so nothing gets missed. In practice, that's what makes menus unreadable — everything shrinks to fit, and nothing stands out.

A better approach:

- **6–8 items per panel**, rotating through categories rather than showing everything at once
- **One clear price size, one clear name size, one clear description size** — three tiers, not five
- **Prices in a consistent spot** (right-aligned is standard) so eyes don't hunt for them
- Let a rotating playlist do the work a cramped single screen can't — [dayparted scheduling](/blog/content-scheduling-for-venue-screens) can show breakfast in the morning and dinner at night instead of everything all the time

## Portrait or landscape?

Portrait suits a long list of items — drink menus, specials boards, anything read top-to-bottom in a line. Landscape suits fewer items with bigger photos — a featured combo, a seasonal drink, a hero image doing most of the selling. If you're mounting a TV specifically as a menu board and haven't bought it yet, this is worth deciding before you mount anything, since flipping orientation later usually means remounting.

## Motion: use it to guide, not to decorate

A gentle crossfade between panels helps a static list feel alive without hurting readability. Scrolling text, spinning graphics, or constant on-screen motion do the opposite — they compete with the words for attention and make the board harder to read, not easier. If you want movement, put it in a hero photo or a subtle background loop, and keep the text itself still while it's on screen.

## When a printed board is honestly fine

Not every board needs to go digital. A three-item drink menu that never changes, a small counter with no daypart shifts, a shop where the owner genuinely doesn't want to touch a dashboard — a well-designed printed sign works fine there, and there's no shame in it. Digital menu boards earn their keep when prices change often, when you run breakfast/lunch/dinner variants, or when the board doubles as a place to run promotions and specials. If none of that applies to you, don't feel pressured to digitize a menu that doesn't need it.

## FAQ

**What font size is too small for a menu board?**
If a customer standing where your line actually forms can't read your prices without stepping closer, it's too small. Test from real distance, not from your desk.

**Should I use a dark or light background?**
Either works as long as the contrast between text and background is high. Dark backgrounds tend to make food photography and prices pop a bit more, but a clean light background works just as well for text-heavy boards.

**How many menu items should be on screen at once?**
6–8 per panel is a reliable ceiling. Rotate categories through a playlist instead of shrinking text to fit everything on one screen.

**Do I need different designs for [cafes](/digital-signage-for/cafes-coffee-shops) versus [restaurants](/digital-signage-for/restaurants)?**
The readability rules are the same everywhere. What changes is pacing — a cafe with a fast line benefits from simpler, faster-scanning layouts, while a sit-down restaurant can afford richer photography and longer dwell time per panel.
