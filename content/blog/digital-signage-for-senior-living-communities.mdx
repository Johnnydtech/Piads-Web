Walk through most senior living and assisted living communities and you'll find a TV in the lobby and another in the dining room, both usually tuned to cable news or a channel nobody picked. Meanwhile, the front desk is re-printing the weekly activity calendar, taping new dining menus to the wall, and fielding the same "what time is bingo?" question for the third time that day.

That's the gap digital signage closes. Communities already have the screens and the WiFi. What's missing is a simple way to put daily schedules, menus, and family-facing content on them — and, for communities open to it, a modest revenue stream from local businesses that want to reach residents' families and caregivers.

## Why senior living communities are a natural fit

**Routine-driven environments.** Life in assisted living runs on a schedule — meals, activities, visiting hours, medication reminders. A screen that always shows "what's happening today" removes a constant source of staff interruptions.

**A captive, attentive audience in common areas.** Residents spend real time in lobbies, dining rooms, and activity rooms — often 30+ minutes at a stretch. That's sustained visibility, not a glance on the way out the door.

**Families and visitors are a second audience.** Adult children, prospective residents touring the community, and visiting friends all pass through the same common areas. Content aimed at them (community updates, upcoming events, "ask us about respite care") reaches a different, equally valuable group than content aimed at residents.

**Low staff bandwidth, high communication needs.** Activities directors and front-desk staff are stretched thin. A screen that updates itself from a pre-built weekly schedule saves real printing and reposting time.

## Where screens work best

| Location | Best Content | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lobby / reception | Daily schedule, community announcements, visiting hours, local ads | First thing families and visitors see |
| Dining room | Today's menu, meal times, dietary notes | Residents are seated and reading while waiting |
| Activity room | Weekly activity calendar, class times, event photos | Reinforces what's happening and when |
| Hallways near common areas | Birthday shout-outs, event recaps, gentle reminders | Passive repetition during normal movement |

If a community is starting with one screen, the dining room usually wins — it's where the most residents sit still the longest, every single day.

## How communities actually use it

### Daily and weekly schedules

Replace the printed activity sheet with a screen that shows today's schedule automatically, with the full week available on request. Update it once; it's current on every screen at once, in every building if the community has more than one.

### Dining menus and meal announcements

Breakfast, lunch, and dinner menus can rotate on a [daypart schedule](/blog/content-scheduling-for-venue-screens) — morning menu shows at breakfast, afternoon activity reminders show after lunch, dinner menu shows before dinner service. No one has to remember to swap a printed sign.

### Family and community engagement

Photos from the last ice cream social, a birthday wall, a welcome slide for new residents — this is the content families notice and screenshot when they visit. It costs nothing to produce and does more for a community's reputation than most paid marketing.

### Local advertiser revenue

This is the part most communities haven't considered, and the one place where an honest caveat matters: not every community wants ads in front of residents, and that's a completely reasonable call. For communities open to it, the advertiser pool here is genuinely relevant rather than random — home health and companion care agencies, hearing and vision clinics, physical and occupational therapy practices, pharmacies with delivery service, estate and elder law attorneys, and medical transportation services all want visibility with exactly this audience and their adult children. With PiAds, every ad requires the community's approval before it ever airs, so there's no surprise content and no loss of control.

## The honest trade-off: this isn't a typical venue screen

Content for residents needs different rules than a retail menu board: larger text, slower slide rotation (8-12 seconds per slide instead of 4-6), high contrast, and no fast-moving video. If a community has only one or two screens and no one with time to build and maintain a thoughtful rotation, a simpler printed calendar next to a single ambient TV may genuinely be the better call for now — digital signage earns its keep once there's a small amount of time each week to manage it, not before.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will families and residents find ads on the screens inappropriate?

Not if the advertisers are chosen thoughtfully. Home health agencies, hearing clinics, pharmacies, and elder law attorneys are relevant and often genuinely useful to residents and their families. PiAds requires venue approval on every ad before it airs, so a community can decline anything that doesn't fit. Communities that would rather run no ads at all can simply leave those slots empty — the content side works exactly the same either way.

### Is this appropriate for memory care units?

Scheduling and dining content works well anywhere. For memory care specifically, keep rotations slower, avoid flashing or fast-cut video, and stick to simple, high-contrast slides. Many communities choose to skip advertiser content in memory care units entirely and reserve it for independent and assisted living common areas.

### Do we need IT staff to manage this?

No. Setup is a guided, four-step process — venue details, pair the screen, add content, go live — and updating a playlist afterward takes a few minutes from any phone or computer. Most communities run this through their activities director or office manager.

### Can different buildings or wings run different schedules?

Yes. Each screen gets its own playlist, so a dining room screen, a lobby screen, and an activity room screen in a different wing can all show different, independently scheduled content from one account.

### How does this compare to just keeping the printed calendar?

A printed calendar is free and requires no setup — that's a real advantage, and for a very small community with one common area, it may be all that's needed. Digital signage starts paying off once a community has more than one printed thing to keep updated (a schedule and a menu board, say) or more than one common area, where one dashboard update replaces several reprints.
