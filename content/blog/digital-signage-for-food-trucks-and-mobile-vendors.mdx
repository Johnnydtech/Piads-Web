A food truck's menu board has one job that a restaurant's doesn't: it has to work on a different street corner every day, sometimes with no outlet in sight and sometimes with a cell signal that comes and goes. A printed menu board handles that fine. A digital one needs a little more planning — but it also does things paper can't, like updating a sold-out item in real time or swapping the whole menu between a lunch spot and a late-night festival gig.

This guide covers what actually works for a truck or cart: power, connectivity, content, hardware, and a straight answer on whether the local-ad-revenue model that works so well for cafes and gyms makes sense on wheels too (short version: not as much — more on that below).

## How a food truck's screen needs differ from a restaurant's

| | Fixed restaurant | Food truck / mobile vendor |
|---|---|---|
| Power | Always-on outlet | 12V truck power, generator, or battery pack |
| Internet | Reliable WiFi | Mobile hotspot or cellular-connected device, sometimes patchy |
| Location | Same spot every day | Different spot daily or even hourly |
| Dwell time | Minutes to an hour+ | Seconds to a couple minutes in line |
| Content changes | Weekly-ish | Daily — sold-out items, new location, event-specific menu |
| Weather exposure | None | Direct sun, rain, heat — a real factor for the screen itself |

The core difference is that a restaurant's screen is set-and-forget between updates. A food truck's screen is something you touch almost every shift.

## Power and connectivity: the part that actually matters

This is where food truck signage lives or dies, and it's worth being blunt about the trade-offs before buying anything.

**Power.** Most trucks already run a generator or have a 12V system for lights and the POS. A small display pulls little enough power to run off either, but a full-size TV and an always-plugged-in streaming box are a worse fit than a tablet or a compact monitor with a built-in battery. If you're mounting a screen in a window or on the side of the truck, plan the power run before you plan the content.

**Connectivity.** A fixed venue's WiFi doesn't move. A truck's does, if it moves at all — many vendors rely on a phone hotspot or a cellular-connected tablet. The practical implication: build your menu changes in before you leave for the day when you have solid WiFi, rather than expecting to push live updates from a parking spot with one bar of signal. Once a player has synced its current playlist, it keeps playing what it has even if the connection drops for a few minutes — it's only a new update that needs a signal.

Given both constraints, a lightweight Android tablet or a browser-based player on an existing device tends to be the realistic choice for most trucks, over a TV-and-streaming-box setup that assumes a wall outlet and steady WiFi.

## What to put on the screen

**Menu content that earns its keep:**
- Today's full menu, with sold-out items pulled the moment you're out — the single biggest advantage over a printed board
- Combo deals and add-ons, which move average order value more than most vendors expect
- A big, legible price list — more useful in a line than a beautifully styled but hard-to-read menu graphic
- Location and hours for today specifically, since "where are you parked" is the most common question a truck gets asked
- Social handles and a loyalty app QR code, shown while people wait in line with nothing else to look at

**What to skip:** autoplaying video with sound, multi-slide rotations that bury the actual menu, and anything that takes more than a glance to read. A line moves fast and people are deciding what to order, not watching a show.

## The honest trade-off: local ad revenue doesn't translate the same way

PiAds built its model around venues with screens that local businesses can pay to advertise on — the venue approves every ad and keeps 70% of what's booked, with the software running at a flat $10 per screen per month. That model works best where people linger: a cafe table, a gym lobby, a salon chair.

A food truck line doesn't give an advertiser that. Dwell time is seconds, not minutes, and the audience changes block by block. If your truck mostly does quick lunch-rush service, don't expect the ad-revenue side to be a meaningful income stream — the screen's real value there is the menu itself: faster lines, higher add-on sales, fewer "wait, you're out of that?" conversations.

Where it changes: food trucks that work festivals, breweries, markets, or events with real dwell time — people standing around for 20+ minutes waiting on multiple vendors — are a genuinely different case. In that setting, the same screen that runs your menu during a normal lunch stop can carry a local ad slot or two during a slower festival shift, the same way a brewery taproom's screen does. It's a bonus in the right setting, not the main reason to put a screen on your truck.

## Getting started

1. Start with one screen showing today's menu and hours — that alone beats a printed board on flexibility
2. Build tomorrow's content before you leave your home base, while you still have reliable WiFi
3. Add a sold-out toggle to your routine — pull the item the moment it's gone
4. If you regularly work events or markets with longer dwell time, consider opening a local ad slot or two on those shifts only

## FAQ

**Does a food truck need a special "outdoor" screen?**
Not always. A screen mounted inside a serving window out of direct rain is fine with a standard display. One mounted fully outside and exposed to weather needs an outdoor-rated or enclosed unit, which costs more but survives the conditions.

**What if there's no WiFi where I'm parked?**
Build and sync your content before you leave for the day. Once synced, most players keep running their current playlist even if the connection drops — you just can't push a brand-new update until you're back on a signal.

**Is Fire TV a good fit for a food truck?**
It can work if the truck has steady power and WiFi, but most vendors get more flexibility from an Android tablet or the browser-based player, which don't depend on a wall outlet.

**Can a food truck actually make money from local ads on its screen?**
Realistically, not much during normal lunch-rush service — dwell time is too short. It's more viable at festivals, markets, or events where people stand around longer, and it's a nice bonus there, not a primary income source.
