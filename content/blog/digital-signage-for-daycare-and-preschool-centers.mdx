Every daycare and preschool has the same daily ritual: a parent hands off their kid, lingers for a second too long, and glances around the lobby looking for reassurance before they leave. That glance is a screen's entire job.

Pickup and drop-off happen twice a day, every day, for as long as a family is enrolled. Add in tours from prospective parents and the occasional wait during late pickup, and a childcare center generates more lobby dwell time per square foot than almost any other small business. Most centers fill that time with a corkboard, a sign-in binder, and a wall of handprint art. A screen does more — and can pay for itself.

## Why daycares are a strong fit for digital signage

**Built-in anxiety, built-in attention.** Parents dropping off a toddler are primed to look for information: is my kid okay, what's today's schedule, did I miss a form deadline. A screen that answers those questions quietly, on loop, gets watched without anyone asking it to be.

**Two guaranteed touchpoints a day.** A gym might see a member twice a week. A daycare sees the same parent twice a day, five days a week. Over a school year that's 400+ impressions per family — far more repetition than most local advertisers ever get elsewhere.

**A tight, trusted local audience.** Parents of young children are a highly sought-after demographic: they're actively spending on pediatric care, kids' activities, photography, and family services, and they trust recommendations that show up inside a place they already trust.

**Low-stakes content, high goodwill.** Unlike a retail screen that's obviously selling something, a daycare screen showing today's lesson theme or a photo from craft time reads as care, not marketing — which makes everything else on it land better too.

## Daycare and preschool signage at a glance

| | Corkboard / paper sign-in sheet | Private Facebook group | Digital signage (lobby screen) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Updates in real time** | No — someone has to reprint | Yes, but parents must check their phone | Yes, update from your phone and it's live instantly |
| **Visible to every parent at drop-off** | Only if they stop and read it | No — depends on who opens the app | Yes — it's the first thing in the room |
| **Shows photos/videos of the day** | Rarely, and only printed | Yes | Yes, continuously |
| **Can generate revenue** | No | No | Yes, via local ad slots |
| **Setup effort** | Low | Low | One afternoon |
| **Ongoing cost** | Printing/time | Free | ~$10/screen/month |

A Facebook group is genuinely fine for sharing the day's photos after pickup — don't feel pressure to replace it. A screen's advantage is different: it's the one channel every parent sees *during* the two minutes they're physically standing in your lobby, whether or not they ever open an app.

## 6 things to put on a daycare lobby screen

### 1. Today's schedule and theme

A simple rotating slide — "Today: Letter 'B' week, outdoor play at 10am, art project: paper plates" — gives parents something to ask their kid about at pickup. It also quietly signals that the day is structured and intentional, which matters a lot during a tour.

### 2. Photos from the day

Nothing reassures a parent faster than seeing their kid's class actually doing something. A rotating slideshow of photos from that morning's activities — snack time, outdoor play, art projects — turns an anxious drop-off into a reassured one, and gives waiting parents something to actually watch instead of their phones.

### 3. Enrollment and tuition reminders

Tuition due dates, re-enrollment deadlines for next year, waitlist openings for a new age group, summer camp sign-ups — all the admin messages that normally go out as emails nobody opens. A screen parents see twice daily is a much higher-reach channel for the same information.

### 4. Safety and pickup procedures

New pickup authorization process, a reminder to sign in on the app, updated drop-off lane flow, a note about an upcoming fire drill. Centers that update these constantly benefit the most from a screen that doesn't need reprinting every time a policy shifts.

### 5. Classroom spotlights and milestones

A "Star of the Week" slide, a birthday shoutout, a note about a class hitting a reading milestone — small, cheap-to-make content that makes parents feel seen and builds the kind of loyalty that drives referrals and five-star reviews.

### 6. Local advertiser revenue

This is the part most centers don't realize is on the table. Families with young kids are exactly who certain local businesses want in front of: pediatric dentists and pediatricians, kids' photographers, birthday party venues, swim schools, tutoring centers, and family-friendly restaurants all want repeat access to the same parents your lobby already sees twice a day.

## FAQ

**Is digital signage worth it for a single-location daycare with one lobby?**
Usually yes, even with one screen. The unique thing about childcare is that you get two guaranteed daily touchpoints per family — a reach-and-frequency combination most small businesses would pay a lot for, and here it's included in the same $10/month screen.

**Will parents find ads in a childcare lobby off-putting?**
Not if they're relevant and approved by you first. A pediatric dentist or a kids' photographer ad fits naturally in a room full of parents; you control which categories of advertisers get approved, and nothing runs without your sign-off.

**Can I use this instead of my sign-in app or parent communication app?**
No, and you shouldn't try to. A screen is a passive, always-on channel for information everyone in the room should see — it complements your sign-in and messaging apps rather than replacing them.

**What if I don't have anyone to manage content regularly?**
Keep it simple at first: a schedule slide, a handful of photos, and a safety reminder on loop is enough to start. Most directors find that updating it once a week, around five minutes of work, keeps it feeling current.

**Do I need separate screens for different classrooms?**
Most centers start with one screen in the shared lobby or entrance, since that's where every family passes through regardless of which classroom their child is in. Multi-room centers sometimes add a second screen near a secondary entrance once the first one proves useful.
