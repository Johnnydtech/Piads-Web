Walk into almost any independent cinema and you'll see the same three screens doing the same three jobs: a showtimes board near the box office, a concession menu above the counter, and a lobby TV nobody's quite sure what to do with. All three are solid digital signage use cases — and none of them require anything close to the specialized, DCI-compliant systems running inside the auditoriums.

This guide is about that second category: the lobby, box office, and concession screens, not the projection booth. If you run a single-screen independent theater or a small regional chain, this is where digital signage pays for itself fastest.

## Where signage fits in a theater

| Location | What it replaces | Best content | Dwell time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Box office / marquee | Printed showtimes letterboard | Showtimes, trailers, ticket pricing | 1–5 min (line) |
| Concession stand | Static menu boards | Combo pricing, upsells, specials | 2–8 min (line) |
| Lobby | Framed posters, a muted cable news TV | Now playing, trailers, local ads, loyalty program | 5–20 min (early arrivals, waiting for a friend) |
| Auditorium pre-show | Third-party ad reels, slideshow loops | Same lobby content, looped before trailers | Full seated audience, 10–15 min |

The box office and concession boards are the easy wins — they're direct upgrades over a letterboard or a laminated menu, with the added benefit of changing instantly when a showtime shifts or a combo price changes. The lobby and pre-show slots are where the advertising upside shows up, because that's where people are standing around with nothing to do but look at a screen.

## Why theaters are good advertising inventory

A cinema lobby has a trait most venues would kill for: a captive, local audience with dead time baked into the experience. People arrive 10–20 minutes before showtime because tickets and trailers tell them to, not because they're in a hurry. That's long dwell time, attached to a screen, for free.

It's also a hyper-local audience in a very specific mindset — they just spent money on entertainment and are about to spend more at the concession stand. Nearby restaurants, [a dinner spot for after the movie](/digital-signage-for/restaurants), ice cream shops, arcades, and even [retail stores](/digital-signage-for/retail-stores) in the same shopping center are natural advertisers, because the audience is already out, already spending, and already nearby.

## What to actually put on the screens

**Box office / marquee board:** Showtimes grouped by film, with runtime and rating visible from the ticket line. Keep font sizes generous — see the [readability rules for menu boards](/blog/digital-menu-board-design-readable-from-10-feet); the same distance-to-text-size math applies to a showtimes board.

**Concession menu:** Combos first, à la carte second, and at least one upsell panel ("add a drink for $2"). Rotating in a popcorn-and-soda combo promo during matinees versus evening shows is a simple [dayparting](/blog/content-scheduling-for-venue-screens) win — matinee crowds skew toward families and bundle deals, evening crowds skew toward date-night upgrades.

**Lobby and pre-show loop:** Now-playing posters and trailers, a loyalty program or punch card plug, upcoming releases, and — once content is covering the basics — local ads. This is also where a theater's own cross-promotion lives: "Buy a $25 gift card, get a free popcorn," member pricing, or a birthday party package.

## Setting it up without touching the projection system

This is the part that surprises most first-time theater owners: lobby and concession signage is a completely separate system from the auditorium's digital cinema server. You're not integrating with anything delicate — it's the same kind of setup as a restaurant's menu board.

- **Hardware:** A TV plus a [Fire TV Stick](/players/fire-tv) handles box office and concession boards reliably; Fire TV is the flagship pick here because it's cheap, holds up to all-day use, and needs no IT support. Lobby TVs that already exist can often just run a signage app directly — see the [full device lineup](/devices) for Android, Raspberry Pi, and browser-based options if a screen has no spare HDMI port to spare.

- **Content:** Showtimes and pricing can be built once as a template and updated in minutes when the schedule changes weekly, which is the actual pain point most theaters are solving when they go digital in the first place.

## The honest trade-off

If a theater only has one showtimes board that rarely changes and a printed menu that's been the same for two years, digital signage is a nice-to-have, not a must. A laminated showtimes sheet reprinted every Monday is a perfectly reasonable system for a tiny single-screen theater with a stable schedule. Where digital earns its cost is multi-film schedules that shift daily, concession pricing that changes with promotions, and any interest at all in running local ads — at that point, the flexibility pays for the $10/month almost immediately.

## FAQ

**Does this replace our digital cinema projection system?**
No. This is strictly for lobby, box office, and concession screens — the equipment that shows showtimes, menus, and ads. The auditorium's projection and DCI-compliant playback system is a completely separate, specialized system that digital signage software doesn't touch.

**Can we show movie trailers on the lobby screen?**
Yes, as long as you have rights to the trailer content you're using — most studio trailers are released for promotional use, but check terms for anything beyond standard public trailers.

**What if our showtimes change daily?**
That's the main case for going digital over print. Update once in the dashboard and every screen reflects it — no reprinting a letterboard every morning.

**Is this worth it for a single-screen independent theater?**
Often yes, mostly for the concession and box office boards — [overall digital signage costs](/blog/how-much-does-digital-signage-cost) are low enough that even a small, single-screen theater usually breaks even on convenience alone, before any ad revenue.

**Do we need design skills to build the showtimes or menu boards?**
No. [Canva has signage-sized templates](/blog/canva-digital-signage-display-on-tv) for menu boards and promotional slides that export straight to a screen — no design background required.
