A single café with one screen is easy. Three cafes, two gyms, or a five-location salon group with a screen in every lobby is a different problem entirely.

The content has to stay on-brand across every site. Someone needs to know, without driving to each location, whether a screen in the back room went dark three days ago. And every location has its own neighborhood, its own regulars, and arguably its own local advertisers — not one generic ad feed piped in from headquarters.

Most owners who grow from one location to several either over-correct (buying an enterprise CMS built for 500-store retail chains) or under-correct (managing each location's screen separately with no shared system at all). Neither fits a 2–10 location small business. Here's what actually does.

## The three approaches, compared

| Approach | Best for | Where it breaks down |
|---|---|---|
| Fully manual, per-location | A single location, or locations run by fully independent owners | No shared branding, no visibility into which screens are offline, duplicate work per site |
| Enterprise CMS | Chains with 50+ locations and a dedicated IT/marketing team | Expensive licensing, long contracts, overkill for a 3–8 location small business |
| Lightweight multi-location signage (PiAds) | 2–10 location small businesses — cafes, gyms, salons, clinics | Not built for thousands of locations, but that's not the problem you have |

If you're running a handful of locations, the enterprise option is solving a scale problem you don't have, and the fully manual option is creating work you don't need to do.

## What should stay centralized

A few things belong at the brand level, managed once and pushed everywhere:

- **Core branding** — logo, colors, fonts, and any evergreen "about us" or loyalty program content
- **Company-wide promotions** — a seasonal campaign or new product launch that applies to every location
- **Screen health monitoring** — one dashboard showing which screens are online, which went offline, and when each was last seen, instead of calling each manager to ask "is the TV working?"

This is also where a native player matters. Running the [Fire TV app](/players/fire-tv) at every location means every screen behaves the same way — same update cadence, same remote management — instead of troubleshooting five different setups five different ways.

## What should stay local

The mistake chains make at this scale is pushing identical content to every screen. A multi-location small business has an advantage a true enterprise chain doesn't: each location is still small enough to feel like a neighborhood business. Keep that.

- **Store hours and local events** — a location near a college campus has a different calendar than one in a suburban strip mall
- **Local staff spotlights** — a new stylist or trainer at one location isn't relevant content at the other four
- **Local advertisers** — the salon downtown and the salon in the next suburb over have different neighbors wanting screen time

That last point is where a lot of multi-location owners leave money on the table. A shared local ad marketplace lets each location pull in ads from businesses actually near *that* site — a yoga studio ad makes sense near your downtown gym, a different local business entirely makes sense near your suburban one. ## Monitoring uptime without visiting every site

The single most common multi-location complaint: a screen goes dark at one location and nobody notices for a week. At one site, that's a minor annoyance. Across five or ten, it's a recurring hole in your marketing and your ad revenue.

Live screen status — online/offline, last-seen timestamp — turns this from a guessing game into a two-minute morning check. You see which screens need attention before a customer or an advertiser notices the blank TV.

## Hardware across multiple locations

Keep hardware decisions simple and repeatable rather than picking a different player for each site:

- **Primary choice:** a [Fire TV Stick](/blog/fire-tv-stick-digital-signage-setup-guide) at every location — cheap, consistent, and avoids a pile of mismatched devices across your sites
- **Backup / fast rollout:** the browser-based web player for a new location opening before hardware arrives, or for a screen you're testing before committing
- **Mixed environments:** if one location already has an Android tablet or a Raspberry Pi from a prior setup, it's fine to run alongside Fire TV devices elsewhere — see the full [player comparison](/devices) for what fits which situation

The goal isn't uniform hardware for its own sake — it's not having to remember five different setup processes when something needs fixing.

## What to skip until you're well past 10 locations

- **Dedicated signage staff** — one person spending 30–60 minutes a week across all locations is enough at this scale
- **Custom integrations** with inventory or POS systems — nice eventually, not necessary to get consistent branding and uptime visibility now
- **A separate IT vendor contract** — if you need a vendor just to keep screens online, the platform is the wrong fit for a business your size

## Getting started across locations

1. Set up your first location fully — branding, content loop, one or two local ad slots
2. Clone the content structure to each new location, swapping in local hours, staff, and advertisers
3. Check the screen health dashboard weekly across all sites instead of calling each manager
4. Open local ad slots at each location independently — a gym group with locations in different neighborhoods will attract different advertisers at each one

## FAQ

**Do I need different software for multi-location digital signage versus a single screen?**
No. The same platform works for one screen or fifty — what changes is how you organize content (centralized branding, localized promotions and ads) rather than the tool itself.

**How do I know if a screen at another location is offline?**
A live screen health dashboard shows online/offline status and last-seen time for every screen across every location, so you don't have to call each site to check.

**Should every location run identical ads?**
No — each location's screens should pull from advertisers relevant to that neighborhood. A shared marketplace with per-location approval lets each site's manager pick ads that fit, while the venue still keeps 75% of the revenue from every booking.

**What hardware works best across multiple locations?**
Standardizing on one device type — a Fire TV Stick at every site is the simplest — avoids juggling different setup processes per location. Mixing in a browser-based player for a new or temporary location is fine.

**At what point does a business need enterprise signage software instead?**
Once you're past roughly 10-15 locations with dedicated IT and marketing staff, enterprise CMS features like role-based permissions at scale start to pay for themselves. Below that, it's more cost and complexity than a small multi-location business needs.
