You have about eight seconds.

That's how long the average person spends looking at a digital screen in a venue — a gym, a coffee shop, a coworking space — before their attention moves on. Eight seconds isn't a lot. But it's more than enough to plant a seed that gets someone through your door, if your ad is built right.

Most local businesses waste those eight seconds with an ad that looks like a cluttered flyer: too many words, a logo that's too small to read, a phone number buried in the corner, and a message that says everything and nothing at once.

This guide is about writing an ad that does the opposite — one that earns those eight seconds and converts them into real customers.

## The Single Most Important Rule: One Message Per Ad

Before you write a word of copy, decide on *one thing* you want to communicate. Not three things. Not five. One.

Is it:
- A specific offer ("20% off your first visit")?
- A specific service ("We fix cracked windshields while you wait")?
- A specific event ("Live jazz every Friday starting at 7pm")?
- A specific problem you solve ("Finally, a gym without a long-term contract")?

The businesses that struggle most with digital signage ads are the ones who try to include their full menu of services. They cram in a logo, a tagline, three services, a phone number, a website, and a "follow us on Instagram" prompt — and the viewer absorbs none of it.

When you narrow to one message, suddenly your ad has room to breathe. The message gets bigger, bolder, and easier to read from across a room. More importantly, it gives the viewer something clear to remember when they leave.

## The Formula That Works

The best venue screen ads share a simple structure:

**Headline → Proof or Detail → Call to Action**

That's it. Here's what each piece does:

**The headline** is your one message, written as a simple, direct statement. It should be readable in two seconds at ten feet away. Aim for five to eight words maximum.

- "Arlington's only 24-hour pet grooming salon"
- "New patient appointments available this week"
- "Scan this to get your first class free"
- "We serve the best banh mi in Clarendon"

Notice what these headlines have in common: they're specific. Not "premium grooming services." Not "high-quality food." Specific claims — locations, availability, bold assertions — stick in the memory. Vague ones don't.

**The proof or detail** is one line that earns the headline. It answers "why should I believe this?" or "what do I need to know to act on this?" Keep it to a single short sentence.

- "Family-owned since 2009 · Rated 4.9 stars on Google"
- "Evening and weekend slots · No referral needed"
- "First class free, no commitment · 7 days a week"
- "Made fresh daily with ingredients from local farms"

**The call to action** tells the viewer exactly what to do next. Be explicit. Don't assume they'll figure it out.

- "Visit us at 3201 Wilson Blvd, Ballston"
- "Scan the QR code to book online"
- "Walk in anytime — no appointment needed"
- "Mention this screen for 10% off"

The cleaner and more direct each of these three pieces is, the better your ad performs.

## What Kills a Venue Screen Ad

**Too much text.** If your ad requires more than four seconds to read, most people won't read it. They glance, move on, and retain nothing. Every line of text you remove makes the remaining lines more powerful.

**Your logo as the headline.** Your business name is important, but it's not a message. The viewer needs to know what you offer, not just who you are. Put your logo in a corner. Give the headline real estate to your offer.

**Small fonts.** A font that looks readable on your laptop screen is often too small to read on a venue display from across a coffee shop. If your headline isn't at least 80-100 points, test it by standing 10 feet back. If you squint, it's too small.

**Low contrast.** White text on a light background. Black text on a dark photo. These seem obvious, but they're everywhere. Your text needs to pop from the background without any effort from the viewer. Dark text on white, or white text on a dark overlay over a photo — that's the reliable choice.

**Generic stock photography.** A smiling model holding a coffee cup next to "Experience the Difference" isn't an ad. It's visual noise. Show your actual product, your actual space, your actual team. Local authenticity beats polished genericness every time.

**A phone number as the main CTA.** Nobody dials a number they see on a screen. They glance at your ad while they're between sets at the gym. Their hands are full. Their phone isn't out. If you need people to contact you, use a QR code linked directly to a booking page or your Google Business profile. Make it effortless.

## Writing Headlines That Actually Work

Here are the patterns that consistently perform for local businesses:

**The specificity play:** Instead of "Quality auto repair," try "Your check engine light diagnosed in 30 minutes or less." Instead of "Great coffee," try "Roasted within 48 hours. Every bag."

**The neighborhood anchor:** Calling out a specific neighborhood creates instant familiarity. "The neighborhood salon Columbia Pike actually talks about" works better than "Arlington's best salon" because it's more precise and more credible.

**The problem-first headline:** Lead with the pain, not the solution. "Tired of overpriced gym memberships with 12-month lock-ins?" lands harder than "Affordable fitness without contracts" — it starts where the customer's frustration is.

**The limited-time hook:** Urgency drives action. "Flash sale: 25% off massages this week only" outperforms "Massage services available" because it gives a reason to act now instead of later.

**The bold claim:** A confident, specific assertion is more memorable than a safe generality. "We guarantee you'll love your cut or your money back" is a headline. "Professional haircuts in a relaxed atmosphere" is not.

## The QR Code Question

If you include a QR code — and you should, if you have a specific URL to drive people to — make it link somewhere useful.

Good destinations:
- Your online booking page
- A specific landing page with the offer from the ad
- Your Google Business profile (to show hours, reviews, and directions)
- A direct menu or product page

Bad destinations:
- Your homepage
- A URL that loads slowly on mobile
- A page that makes users hunt for the offer they saw on the screen

The QR code is only as good as where it goes. If clicking through is confusing or the offer isn't immediately obvious on the landing page, you'll lose the conversion you just earned.

## A Tale of Two Ads

Here's the same business, done two ways:

**Version A (common, ineffective):**
> *Sunrise Acupuncture & Wellness Center*
> *Traditional Chinese Medicine · Acupuncture · Cupping · Herbal Remedies · Reiki*
> *Serving the Arlington community since 2015*
> *Call us at (703) 555-0142 or visit www.sunriseacupuncture.com*
> *Follow us on Instagram @sunriseacupunctureva*

**Version B (focused, effective):**
> *Stress. Insomnia. Chronic pain.*
> *Acupuncture that actually works — ask the 200+ Arlington patients who've tried it.*
> *New patient special: first session $65. Scan to book.*

Version A is a business card. Version B is a conversation. One of them will be remembered when the viewer is lying awake at 2am with a sore back.

## Testing Your Ad Before It Goes Live

You don't need fancy tools to test your copy. Two quick checks work well:

**The phone test:** Take a photo of your ad on a screen (or mock it up at full resolution) and hold your phone 3 feet away. Read it. Everything you can't read easily should be bigger.

**The "so what?" test:** Read your headline out loud. Then ask: "so what?" If the answer is "I don't know, who cares," rewrite it. If the answer is "I should check that place out," you're done.

## The Bigger Picture

Writing a good venue screen ad is the same skill as writing any good direct-response copy: make one clear promise, deliver enough proof to be credible, and make it effortless to take the next step.

The difference with venue screens is the format. You're competing with ambient noise, not the entire internet. Your audience is already local, already out, already spending money. They don't need to be convinced that your category matters — they need to be convinced that your specific business is worth a visit.

Give them one reason. Make it compelling. Make it easy to act on. That's the whole game.
