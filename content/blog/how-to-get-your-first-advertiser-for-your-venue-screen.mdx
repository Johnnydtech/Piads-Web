Setting up a screen to accept ads is the easy part. The question that actually stalls most venues is simpler and harder: who's going to pay to run an ad on it, and how do you get that first "yes"?

The good news is that your first advertiser is usually closer than you think — and landing them isn't about cold outreach to strangers. It's about recognizing who already wants to reach the people standing in your venue, and giving them an easy way to say yes.

## Why the first booking is the hard one

Every venue owner who's turned their screen into a revenue stream describes the same arc: the first ad takes a little effort to land, and every one after that gets easier. That's because a filled screen is proof — once one local business sees an ad running on your TV, it's a much easier sell to the next one, and your own marketplace listing starts doing some of the work for you.

So the goal for booking #1 isn't to build a perfect sales pitch. It's to find the lowest-friction advertiser possible and get something live.

## Start with businesses that already send you customers (or vice versa)

Before approaching strangers, look at who you already have a relationship with:

| Advertiser type | Why they say yes fast |
|---|---|
| A business next door or across the street | They already see your foot traffic and want a piece of it |
| A supplier or vendor you buy from | They want visibility with your exact customer base |
| A business that refers customers to you (or you to them) | The relationship already exists — this just formalizes it |
| A regular customer who also runs a business | They're already a fan; this is an easy ask |
| A local service pro (dentist, realtor, accountant) near your venue | They advertise everywhere already and understand hyperlocal value |

A gym can approach the physical therapy office down the block. A cafe can approach the boutique next door. A salon can approach a nearby nail bar that isn't a direct competitor. None of this requires a sales background — it's just naming the obvious overlap out loud.

## What to actually say

Keep the pitch to three sentences. Something like:

> "We just set up a screen that runs ads from local businesses, and I thought of you first. It's $X for the month, you keep full control over the ad you send, and it runs right where our customers are sitting. Want me to send the details?"

That's it. No deck, no contract to negotiate — on PiAds, the advertiser books and uploads their own ad through the marketplace, and you approve it before it ever airs. You're not selling advertising services; you're offering a slot and a price.

## Pricing your first slot so it's an easy yes

Don't anchor your first booking to what you think the screen is "worth" long-term. Price it low enough that saying yes is a non-decision — you can always raise the price once there's demand. A reasonable starting range:

- **Quiet daypart, lower-traffic venue:** $5–10/day
- **Peak daypart (lunch rush, Saturday morning), decent traffic:** $10–20/day
- **High-traffic venue, prime daypart:** $20–40/day

See our [full pricing guide](/blog/how-much-to-charge-for-venue-screen-ads) for how to price beyond the first booking, and [how many ad slots to actually offer](/blog/how-many-ad-slots-venue-screen-ratio-guide) so ads don't crowd out your own content.

## Honest trade-off: don't expect it to fill itself

A screen that's simply turned on and listed in a marketplace won't generate bookings on its own, especially in the first few weeks. The venues that land a first advertiser fastest are the ones that make one direct ask rather than waiting passively. Once that first ad is live, word travels through a small business community faster than you'd expect — but somebody has to make the first move.

It's also fine if the first slot sits empty for a few days after you ask. Nothing is lost: your own content keeps running either way, and a "no" from one business just means the next conversation is with someone else on the list above.

## Where to find advertisers once your network runs out

Once you've asked everyone you already know, the marketplace itself becomes the source. Local businesses actively browse for nearby screens to book — [see how local businesses advertise on venue screens near them](/blog/how-to-advertise-on-local-venue-screens) for what they're looking for when they find yours. Being listed with accurate venue details (foot traffic, hours, audience) matters here: an advertiser scanning a list of nearby screens picks the one that reads like a clear fit for their business.

## FAQ

**How long does it usually take to get a first advertiser?**
It varies widely, but venues that make a direct ask to one or two nearby businesses in their first week tend to land a booking within that same week. Passively waiting for the marketplace to surface a buyer usually takes longer.

**Do I need a media kit or sales materials?**
No. A short, specific ask to a business that already knows you works better than a formal pitch deck. Save the polish for once you have several advertisers and want to raise your rates.

**What if the first advertiser's ad doesn't look professional?**
You approve every ad before it airs, so nothing low-quality has to go live. Most first-time local advertisers send a simple photo or graphic with their logo, hours, and offer — that's enough.

**Should I give my first advertiser a discount?**
It's common and reasonable to offer a lower introductory rate for the first booking, especially to a business you have a relationship with. You can raise the rate for the next renewal once the slot has proven it gets seen.

**Can I turn down an advertiser I don't want on my screen?**
Yes, always. You control every approval, and there's no obligation to accept a booking from a business that isn't a fit for your venue or customers.
